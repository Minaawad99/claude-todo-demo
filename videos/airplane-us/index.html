A jumbo jet can weigh almost a million pounds.

And it just floats on air. Like, literally air.

No magic, no anti-gravity. Just a really smart wing.

Every plane is fighting four forces at once.

Weight pulls it down. Lift pushes it up.

Thrust shoves it forward. Drag holds it back.

To fly, lift has to beat weight, and thrust has to beat drag.

Let's start with thrust: the jet engines.

And get this, they run the same routine as your car engine.

Suck, squeeze, bang, blow.

A giant fan up front sucks in tons of air.

Compressor blades squeeze it, fuel sprays in, and it explodes.

That blast shoots out the back and spins a turbine, which keeps the whole thing turning.

But here's the twist: most of the push actually comes from that big fan.

Now the plane's racing down the runway at around 180 miles an hour.

And that's where the wing comes in.

Look at it from the side. It's curved on top and tilted up a little.

So as air hits it, the wing shoves that air down.

Push air down, and the air pushes you up. That's lift.

Plus, the air rushing over the curved top speeds up, and its pressure drops.

So you've got high pressure under the wing and low pressure on top.

The wing basically gets sucked upward.

Ever stuck your hand out a car window and tilted it? Same deal.

Tilt it up, and boom, your hand flies.

For takeoff, the wings even slide out extra panels called flaps.

They make the wing bigger and curvier, so you get more lift at low speed.

Then the pilot pulls back, and the nose tips up.

But how does it steer up there?

Ailerons on the wings tip it left or right.

The elevator on the tail points the nose up or down.

And the rudder swings it side to side.

Up at 35,000 feet, the air is way thinner, so there's way less drag.

And here's the wild part: a Boeing 787's wings can flex up about 25 feet.

So next time you fly, remember, you're basically surfing on air.
