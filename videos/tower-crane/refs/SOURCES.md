# Sources — «تعمل ونش برجي إزاي؟»

Script and drawings are our own, in the channel's «لو» build-along style.

Facts checked:
- Foundation: a typical tower-crane pad is about 30 × 30 × 4 ft (~10 × 10 × 1.2 m) and about 400,000 lb (~180 t), with large
  anchor bolts set deep in the concrete (HowStuffWorks "How Tower Cranes Work"; ny-engineers.com blog "The role of tower
  cranes in high-rise building projects"). Real pads come from the manufacturer's loads per crane.
- A mobile crane lifts the first mast sections, jib and counterweights into place (heavyequipmentcollege.edu, "How tower
  cranes are put together").
- Counterweight example: a crane lifting up to ~19.8 t against ~20 t of concrete counterweight blocks (HowStuffWorks).
- Load moment: allowable load falls as the trolley moves out along the jib; load-moment / overload limiters cut the hoist and
  trolley-out motion when the limit is passed (standard tower-crane safety devices).
- Climbing: hydraulic rams in a climbing frame push the slewing unit up ~20 ft (6 m); the crane lifts a 6 m mast section into
  the gap (HowStuffWorks).
- Tall cranes above free-standing height are tied to the building with steel collars/ties.
- Out of service the slew brake must be released so the jib weathervanes freely with the wind; HSE investigated incidents
  where the brake was left on and the crane was damaged (HSE RR1172 and safety bulletin "Tower crane slew brakes"; CPA TCIG
  TIN 038).

Visual references: drawn from the well-known real shape of a hammerhead (saddle-jib) tower crane: lattice mast of square
sections, slewing ring with cab on one side, A-frame/tower head with pendant ties, long lattice jib with trolley and hook
block, short counter-jib with stacked concrete counterweight slabs and the hoist winch, red aviation light and anemometer on
the top.

Script v2 (after the user's reference video, a rope-over-pulley start built up part by part):
- Load falls with radius: e.g. Liebherr 160 EC-B8 Litronic, 8 t max, 60 m jib, ~1.85 t at the tip (cranepedia.com spec page).
- In-service wind: tower cranes are commonly designed for a max in-service wind of 20 m/s (72 km/h) (WorkSafe QLD alert citing
  AS 1418.4; CPA TIN 020); the anemometer warns the operator; many sites stop earlier.
