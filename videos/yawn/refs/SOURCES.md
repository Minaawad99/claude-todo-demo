## Drawings
- **Skull and jaw:** "Human skull side simplified (bones).svg" (Wikimedia Commons) and Gray's Anatomy plate 176, the
  mandible (public domain), downloaded as 960px thumbnails, looked at only. They gave the head's side profile with the
  cranium, the upper jaw and teeth, and a one-piece mandible (body + ramus) that hinges at the condyle, just in front
  of the ear. In the drawing the jaw turns about that point (about 25°) and slides forward a little, as in a real yawn.
- **A yawn:** "Yawning baby.jpg" (Commons): mouth wide open, eyes squeezed shut, head back.
- Wikimedia limited requests (429) during the session. The dog, the macaque, the mouse and the ultrasound were drawn from
  their known shapes: a short-coated dog in profile with a floppy ear, a curled tongue and teeth showing; a macaque with
  a pink face, brown fur and big canines; a grey mouse with whiskers; a grey ultrasound fan widening downward from the
  probe at the top, with the fetus in profile.

## Facts checked
- A human yawn lasts about 6 s on average (Gallup et al. 2016, *Biology Letters*: across 19–24 species, yawn length
  rises with brain size and neuron count. Humans are among the longest, mice among the shortest).
- Fetal yawning is seen on ultrasound from about week 11.
- Oxygen: Provine, Tate & Geldmacher 1987. Breathing 100% O₂, or air high in CO₂, did not change how often people yawned.
- Brain cooling: Gallup & Gallup 2007. A cold pack on the forehead, or breathing through the nose, cut contagious yawning.
  Yawning is linked to sleep–wake transitions, when brain temperature shifts.
- Contagion: stronger between kin and friends than strangers (Norscia & Palagi 2011). Rare before about age 4–5.
- Dogs catching human yawns: Joly-Mascheroni et al. 2008 (21 of 29 dogs); more with the owner (Romero et al. 2013).
- Why yawns are contagious is still unsettled. The script says so.
