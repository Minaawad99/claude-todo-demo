Before we start, there's one small rule. Keep it in mind.
Every plane in the air has four forces pulling it four different ways.
Down is weight, up is lift, forward is thrust, and backward is drag.
So where does weight come from? Gravity pulls the plane down, with its passengers, bags, and fuel.
That's why pilots work out the weight of every flight before takeoff.
What about thrust? The engine sucks in huge amounts of air at the front and blasts it out the back, really fast.
That air shoves the engine forward, like a balloon zooming off when you let it go.
And drag? The air itself rubs against the plane and gets in its way.
The faster it goes, the more drag it gets. That's why it's smooth and pointy.
And lift? That's the wing's job.
The wing is curved on top and tilted a little, so it bends the air flowing over it downward.
The air pushes back and shoves the wing up, like your hand out of a car window when you tilt it.
Above the wing the air moves faster with lower pressure, and below the pressure is higher. Same story, different angle.
So when does it take off? When thrust beats drag, the plane goes faster and faster.
That speed grows the lift until it beats the weight, at around a hundred and eighty miles an hour.
Up high, when all four forces balance out, the plane cruises at a steady speed and height.
Lots of people think the engines are what lift the plane. Nope.
The engines only give it speed. The wing is what turns that speed into lift.
Bottom line: weight pulls down, the wing lifts, the engines push, the air drags, and the plane flies when lift and thrust win.
And the weirdest part? If both engines quit up high, the plane glides down gently like a paper plane, sometimes for more than sixty miles.
And that's the thing about how planes fly.
